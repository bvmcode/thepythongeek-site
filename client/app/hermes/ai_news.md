## Chinese frontier models carry Blackwell's fingerprint in their own weights

Will DePue, an ex-OpenAI researcher who runs the small research shop Unemployment Labs, published a forensic analysis claiming six models from DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax and Ant Group were initialized on export-controlled Blackwell GPUs — and fresh write-ups from TechCrunch and The Register moved the claim into the mainstream on Friday. The method is the interesting part: PyTorch initializes weights through a seeded pseudorandom generator (Philox) run across GPU threads via cuRAND, so a model's starting values depend deterministically on how many parallel threads a chip's layout spawns. DePue searched more than six trillion combinations of chip layout and seed number against published open weights, and the matches are specific: DeepSeek-V4-Flash on B200/B300-class silicon, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Kimi K3 and K3.1-Pro plus MiniMax-M3 on liquid-cooled GB200/GB300 rack-scale systems, while earlier checkpoints from the same labs match Hopper instead. It is also the first public tie of MiniMax and Ant Group to Blackwell. He published seed values so anyone can reproduce the DeepSeek-V3-to-Hopper and V4-Flash-to-Blackwell matches, and says senior researchers inside frontier labs have verified them independently. The caveats are stated plainly: a fingerprint proves hardware access, not chip location — offshore rental stays the loophole, with Epoch AI estimating some two million advanced chips reached China in 2024-2025 via destination-hiding schemes and Malaysia importing an estimated 275,000. If GPUs really leave a signature in what they train, open-weight releases become audit evidence.

## FBI FLASH: an open-source agentic pentesting framework is driving intrusions at machine speed

The FBI's FLASH-20261113-001 reads like the formal end of the era in which agentic hacking was a demo threat: since at least August 2026, malicious actors have used ARTEX — an open-source, Go-written, multi-agent penetration-testing platform — to automate intrusions "at machine speed, from reconnaissance through data exfiltration" across financial services, government, defense, semiconductors, energy, healthcare, manufacturing, telecom and legal. The advisory's headline stat, credited to Microsoft, is that some intrusions went from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours. ARTEX ships no model of its own; it plugs into external LLM APIs, and Zscaler and Check Point analysis cited by the FBI says attacker deployments lean on Chinese-origin models, led by the DeepSeek Flash family, chosen for capability, cost and few observable guardrails when the work is framed as authorized testing. The alert consolidates observed campaigns including exfiltration of personal data on roughly 67,000 people from South Korean financial institutions and a ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain compromise that reached more than 2,250 downstream tenants. The provenance is its own lesson: built by a Chinese security engineer ("Autumn-27"), 1,500-2,000 GitHub stars in ten weeks, repository pulled October 8 after abuse reports — yet still circulating through forks and a commercial ARTEX PRO, with Censys counting 1,600-plus exposed consoles and CrowdStrike tracking one controller burning 1.4 billion input tokens to direct simultaneous intrusions across 200-plus organizations in 14 countries. The mitigations are unglamorous: audit peripheral internet-facing systems, take admin planes off the public internet, least-privilege consent grants, and alert on outbound LLM-API calls and token-billing anomalies — the one footprint agents can't easily hide.

## The FAA ground stop: the AI-tool suspicion rises and falls, the accountability gap stays

Friday's nationwide ground stop — the first since January 2023, with roughly 8,200 cancellations by Friday evening and 3,000-plus Saturday per Cirium — landed days after the FAA finished expanding SMART, an AI-assisted routing system built by Air Space Intelligence under a contract just shy of $1 billion, to every en route control center. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy initially cited a "communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers," Air Space Intelligence issued a flat denial that its tool was involved, and a Homeland Security official, asked on live television about the timing, would only say "We're looking at that." By Saturday the AI suspicion had mostly collapsed under detail: Duffy said a router overloaded a critical communications system while contractor L3Harris converted copper to fiber at a remote site during routine maintenance, and FAA administrator Bryan Bedford said there is "zero evidence of anything AI-related," with a root-cause analysis still running. What survives the all-clear is telling: Fortune reported the disruption so alarmed the White House that the Situation Room and national-security apparatus activated, the controllers' union says it was never consulted on the AI rollout, and Microsoft simultaneously insisted its separate worldwide Outlook/Teams/Copilot outage had no connection to the stop. An AI system was live everywhere right before everything grounded, and the public has no way to inspect either system — the reassurance rests entirely on the parties being scrutinized.

## ChatGPT Wallet lets agents pay without ever seeing the card

OpenAI switched on ChatGPT Wallet on Friday, a real payment rail for its agents built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa. Scope starts narrow: US Pro subscribers aged 18 and over with eligible US-issued cards. Users add a card once; before each purchase they review the items, total and card, then approve with Face ID or fingerprint, leaving a record of what the agent was cleared to buy. Upon approval, the payment network issues a token standing in for the real card — its own number, security code and expiry — so neither the agent nor the merchant ever sees the actual card, while users keep rewards and dispute protections and merchants keep pricing, returns and customer service. OpenAI is taking no cut for now, and its monetization product lead Chaya Nayak framed the small launch as deliberate containment-learning. The plumbing got as much attention as the product: fraud partners Forter, Riskified, Sardine and Signifyd; payment partners Adyen, Checkout.com and Stripe; and a playbook so merchants don't accidentally firewall legitimate agent traffic. The headwinds were named too — United Airlines said Wednesday it will block agents, Amazon stops them at checkout, Schwab and JPMorgan block agents from moving money, and Visa's November test of 22 models as shoppers found 42.8 percent of purchases failed. Agents can technically buy things now; whether the merchant layer lets them is the open question.

## DeepSeek opens its app's whole toolbox to developers

DeepSeek opened the features behind its consumer app to API developers on November 13. Web and image search with citations, a deep-reasoning search mode, file input (text extraction or layout parsing for PDFs, Office documents and images), URL reading and a basic safety check are all enabled by parameters on the deepseek-flash model — the DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 checkpoint — across its Messages, Responses and Chat Completions endpoints, with DeepSeek's servers making the tool calls and merging results into the model's context. The pieces are also exposed as standalone Search, Fetch, OCR, ASR and TTS APIs for developers who want manual control over when tools run. Tool pricing stacks on top of token charges: search and fetch at $0.75 per 1,000 calls each, file parsing and OCR at $0.225 per 1,000 pages, and the voice endpoints at half price as a launch promotion. Two strings attached: public-facing apps built on the API must provide their own safety checks, and the full set currently runs only on deepseek-flash — the heavier V4-Pro doesn't support it. DeepSeek also shipped an open-source chat-demo reference client that imports conversation history from its app, which makes the pitch explicit: a developer can now rebuild the entire DeepSeek consumer experience — search, documents, voice — against one cheap modular API.

## Claude reads Gödel's shorthand — and the Coq proof assistant checks the results

The Guardian's Friday profile of Jan von Plato is the best agentic-workflow story of the week, with formal verification doing the QA. The Helsinki logician is one of roughly six people alive who can read Gabelsberger, the obsolete German shorthand Kurt Gödel used in 16 workbooks — 1,009 pages written in the early 1940s — and since starting the project in 2017 on a €2.5m ERC grant he averaged about a page a week. In August, at a conference on AI and theorem proving, researchers pointed him at Claude; he fed it his transcriptions plus six books he had written on Gödel and other secondary literature, and within days had a 650-page commentary he then controlled "page for page." The headline find: Claude reconstructed Gödel's proof of the independence of the axiom of choice — one definition wrong, the right one sitting two years earlier in his notes — and the reconstruction passed the Coq proof assistant. Asked to extract every theorem Gödel had proved, sketched or glimpsed, Claude returned 663 candidates; run through Coq, about 400 verified as stated, roughly 200 needed repair, and about 50 were false (Gödel himself occasionally scrawled falsch! in the margins). Von Plato says the workbooks contain a dozen-plus themes in logic untouched in the 80-plus years since they were written. The honest caveat belongs to Notre Dame's Joel David Hamkins: "how much is Claude and how much is Gödel is a job for the historians."
