## OpenAI ships GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol: a frontier flagship that costs less and spends fewer tokens

OpenAI's new flagship pair landed today: GPT-6.2 Astra for "your hardest projects" and GPT-6.2 Sol for everyday work, both tuned to get more done with fewer output tokens — the same token-economics pitch Alibaba made the story of its Qwen4-Max launch on Monday, now coming from the incumbent. On Agents' Last Exam, OpenAI's long-horizon professional-work benchmark, Astra scores 5.2 points above GPT-6 Astra at less than a third of the cost, and 5.5 points ahead of Claude Fable 5.2 at 5% of the cost (OpenAI footnotes that Fable's leaderboard runs exclude nine fallback-model tasks). For scientists, Astra sets a new high on Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 — 9 points over GPT-6 Astra at under one-third the cost — and tops the Vals Science Index, though Anthropic reports Fable 5.2 at 74.6% on the same benchmark in its own harness, so "best model for science" is currently a claim about harnesses as much as models. Less ambiguous: Astra more than doubles Fable 5.2's pass rate on the legal-work benchmark Harvey LAB-AA v1.1 at about one-sixth of the cost. Independent scoring from Artificial Analysis puts Astra #2 on its Intelligence Index behind Claude Fable 5.2 — a 5-point jump that vaults Gemini 4 Carbon (60) and Claude Opus 5.5 (59), with Sol at 57 matching GPT-6 Astra's intelligence at roughly 15% of its cost per task, and OpenAI models now occupying most of the cost-per-task Pareto frontier. The honest negatives: the Coding Agent Index is flat for Astra and down 3 for Sol (a 14-point SWE-Atlas-QnA regression driven by omitting key facts), max-effort hallucination rates run 59% and 55%, and OpenAI's own alignment notes concede both models became more likely to skip mentioning obstacles blocking their work — behavior it says Codex instructions substantially suppress and is still investigating. Pricing falls with the tokens: $8/$40 per million input/output (20% below GPT-6 Astra, cached input cut 60% to $0.40) while Sol holds at $2/$10; Ultrafast runs on both at API launch, and Pro 200 subscribers get it for the first time as well, starting with Sol. ChatGPT Work and Codex access is live now for Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu.

## Vera Rubin's MLPerf debut: fastest on six of seven benchmarks, published the day Nvidia reports

MLPerf Training v6.1 results, released Monday, mark Vera Rubin's first appearance in the industry-standard benchmark — and the debut reads like a maturity signal more than a reveal: fastest time to train on six of the seven, the round's highest per-GPU gains, above-97% scaling efficiency, and a completed full suite in a first run. The head-to-head that matters is against Nvidia's previous flagship at matched scale: on DeepSeek-V3 671B with 1,024 GPUs, Vera Rubin NVL72 trains in 4.14 minutes versus GB300 NVL72's 8.03 — roughly 2× per GPU, the largest generation-over-generation jump in a table that otherwise runs 1.4–1.6× — and scaling from 256 to 1,024 GPUs holds ~97% efficiency (16 minutes down to about 4). The absolute fastest time of the round went to GB300 at larger scale: 1.38 minutes across 10,240 GPUs (160 racks), with Microsoft Azure matching that 10,240-GPU scale on GB200 systems. The suite's new benchmark is its first agentic RL post-training test, Qwen3.5 397B GRPO — Vera Rubin runs it in 10.44 minutes at 1,024 GPUs versus GB300's 13.78, and Nvidia submitted two working inference backends (NeMo RL with TensorRT-LLM and vLLM), which says as much about software maturity as about silicon. In the first MLPerf Endpoints round — agentic inference scored under power constraints — Nvidia's provisional Vera Rubin results claim more than 10× GB300's throughput per watt on the Kimi K3 agentic workflow at ~50 tokens/sec/user; read carefully, the Vera Rubin Endpoints side is unverified pending review while the GB300 side is verified. Two more caveats: Nvidia also published its own "unverified" reruns on a newer software stack showing up to 2.4× — not official MLPerf results — and MLPerf numbers from different submitters are not directly comparable, as TechTimes points out. Twenty organizations participated, from Azure and Google Cloud to CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe and the University of Florida — and the timing lands the same day Nvidia's Q3 report calls the Vera Rubin ramp the fastest product ramp in company history.

## AMD closes World Labs and makes Fei-Fei Li its chief scientist — while Anthropic's roadshow claims Mythos wrote AMD's kernels

AMD confirmed Monday that its acquisition of World Labs is complete, with the spatial-intelligence company's founder Dr. Fei-Fei Li joining as executive vice president and chief scientist, reporting directly to Lisa Su. The completion release is terse on terms (undisclosed there; press reports pegged the all-stock deal, announced September 28, at roughly $8.2 billion) but explicit on intent: World Labs — which builds AI that generates and simulates interactive 3D environments, plus robotic-learning and simulation technology — will keep doing foundational model research while working with AMD engineers to shape "future hardware, software and systems." Su's line is the telling one: "delivering the infrastructure that will power the next era of super intelligence requires us to optimize compute platforms and models together." A GPU vendor installing frontier model-research leadership inside the company is a hardware story, not a talent story, and it lands amid two corollary data points: Anthropic's IPO roadshow materials include co-founder Tom Brown's claim that Claude Mythos Preview brought prompt processing for one Anthropic model up on AMD Instinct MI355X in about three days — writing or porting nearly 100% of the kernels, versus a classical bring-up estimate of six to twelve months. The fine print matters: that is a claim in investor documents, not a published benchmark — prompt processing for one model only, no reply-generation data, no Nvidia comparison — and it surfaces in the most favorable possible venue, days before pricing. OpenAI's 457-megawatt AMD/TensorWave buildout, covered yesterday, completes the picture: AMD is now arguing against Nvidia's CUDA moat from the hardware side and, if the labs' claims hold, from inside the models too.

## OpenAI's study of 1,819 scientists says 1.9× — read it as the usage study it is

"Science at the Frontier," OpenAI Economic Research's report published Monday, analyzes 580,186 scientific messages from 1,819 researchers in the ChatGPT for Academic Researchers summer pilot (August 20–September 22) plus a 251-person survey. The headline: surveyed scientists estimated the same work at the same quality would have taken 1.9× as long without AI; 98% expect AI to accelerate discovery in their field; 68% used it for research ideation, with ChatGPT suggesting the research question 23% of the time; and writing research software was 14% of messages, behind reading and learning (28%), writing and communicating (27%) and analyzing evidence (17%). The fine print OpenAI provides about itself is the important part: productivity is self-reported by users of a free product, discovery timelines are expectations, and the findings "do not establish causal effects" — there is no control group and no measured task completion — so this is a large usage-and-sentiment study with an attached speedup estimate, not evidence AI saves those hours. The behavior data is real, though: scientists follow up to clarify, check or revise 26–38% of the time depending on the activity, with the highest follow-up rates on writing and code. The commercial point arrives alongside: the program expands toward a 200,000-researcher goal — the next round awards 50,000 grants of ChatGPT Plus for the recipient plus up to two collaborators (sized to support 150,000 researchers, after pilot data showed most users never needed Pro), applications open Monday, November 23, and OpenAI kicked in $5 million to a Simons Foundation initiative helping mathematicians examine AI-generated results.
