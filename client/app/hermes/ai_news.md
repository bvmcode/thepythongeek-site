## Google ships Gemini 4 Neon: frontier-grade work at midsize prices

Google's newest Gemini 4 model landed today with one job: frontier-level output at midsize-model cost. Gemini 4 Neon is priced at $3 per million input tokens ($0.15 cached — a 95% discount) and $15 per million output, with a 1 million-token context and four thinking levels that scale up to a new xhigh tier for the hardest engineering, analytical and scientific work. The independent scores make the pitch concrete: #3 overall on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index with 61 at xhigh — behind only larger, slower models — #2 on the Vals Index (70.9%) while sitting on the cost-quality Pareto frontier, and at its default level 32% cheaper per task than Gemini 4 Carbon, with roughly 50–80% lower per-task cost than competing frontier models. It takes #1 on Zapier's AutomationBench (56.3%), Andon Labs' Vending-Bench 2 ($16,843) and the Vals Legal Research and Tax Agent benches, and debuts #2 on Arena's new Agent Arena Index (1336 across 4,200+ real sessions) with #2 steerability. It's best-in-class on Terminal-Bench Science (70.5%) and RiemannBench (84.0%), and in code it leads the AA Coding Agent Index (58.6) with 65.2% on Terminal-Bench 4.1 and a SWE-sweep that triples from 12.8% on the mini-SWE harness to 39.0% inside Antigravity, where it can also orchestrate Hydrogen subagents. A dedicated cyber tier posts state-of-the-art 90.5% on SEC-bench Pro and 51.26% on WebExploitBench at about half the cost of larger models, with Fairwind defender access rolling out in the coming days. The numbers worth noticing for an agentic model are the honesty ones: 0.0% cheating on Terminal-Bench 4.0, a 2.45% shortcut rate (the lowest of any Gemini 4 model), 99.2% on Google's agent-honesty eval, and full resistance on Gray Swan's indirect-prompt-injection benchmark at its highest setting. Glean's early evaluation matched or exceeded Carbon on quality at 3× the speed and 60% of the cost. A day after OpenAI launched GPT-6.2 on a cheaper-per-task pitch, the frontier race is now formally priced per task — and Google's answer spends less without leaving the top three.

## AI security research goes autonomous: a model found four of five nginx RCEs in 90 minutes

F5 shipped emergency patches for ten nginx vulnerabilities today after one of the most crowded coordinated disclosures in the web server's history: Xint, Wiz, Rapid7, Ikotas Labs and Metnir independently reported flaws in software that runs roughly 225 million sites — about a third of the web. Two are critical 9.2-CVSS unauthenticated RCEs that require only HTTP/2: CVE-2026-123007, an integer underflow in HTTP/2 flow-control accounting that turns into an out-of-bounds memory copy and has sat in the code since nginx 1.9.5 introduced HTTP/2 in September 2015, and CVE-2026-123633, a use-after-free on HTTP/2 connection reuse that has lurked about six years. Xint computes that 98.9% of hosts with identifiable versions are exposed, and its research is the part that matters for AI: its OpenAI-powered system — GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Luna — scanned about 50,000 lines of nginx source in roughly 90 minutes for about $1,000 of tokens and returned four of the five "nginx2root" bugs with no human steering (the fifth came from a human researcher), then chained one with a separate Linux kernel SCTP flaw to reach root, with recorded kills on AWS, Azure, GCP and bare metal. Wiz found both HTTP/2 bugs independently with its Atlas AI-scanning harness, Rapid7 published a working proof of concept, and the fixes are out in nginx 1.30.7 and 1.31.9; disabling HTTP/2 blocks only the two HTTP/2 flaws. No in-the-wild exploitation has been seen, but working exploits exist — and Xint CEO Brian Pak's point is the strategic one: AI-enabled attackers reverse-engineer from the smallest disclosure clues, so the decades-old disclosure playbook is changing. It's also the week's second AI-assisted critical find in foundational software, following the Java flaw AI surfaced earlier in the week.

## Microsoft's agent SDK was hijacked on PyPI — a credential stealer that runs whenever Python starts

Microsoft's official agent-framework package — the open-source toolkit for building AI agents, near 200,000 downloads a month — was hijacked on PyPI on Tuesday evening: malicious versions 1.26.1 (uploaded 20:35 UTC) and 1.26.2 (21:08 UTC, published by an account created that same day) carried a credential stealer whose payload lives entirely in packaging. Each installs a .pth startup hook, so the collector fires on every Python interpreter start — even if agent-framework is never imported — while the genuine framework still installs and works normally, hiding the compromise. It harvests environment variables matching TOKEN, SECRET, KEY, AZURE, AWS, GITHUB and OPENAI, reads up to 60 credential files (Azure CLI, Git, Docker, npm/pip configs, Hugging Face tokens, .env files) and scrapes the environments of up to 300 running processes. Then it mints fresh tokens: Azure managed-identity tokens across a dozen resource audiences (Resource Manager, Graph, Key Vault, Storage, AI services, Azure DevOps), Azure DevOps federation tokens, and — in 1.26.2 — GitHub Actions OIDC tokens, which matter because an Entra ID federated credential that trusts the workflow can be exchanged for Azure access with no stored secret at all. Everything posts, zlib-compressed, to a webhook.site endpoint. PyPI quarantined 1.26.1, but the attacker still held publish rights and shipped the renamed 1.26.2 minutes later; both were gone by 21:30 UTC, leaving 1.26.0 (November 12) as the last clean release. Security firms caught both releases within minutes; Microsoft hasn't said how its publish access was taken. Remediation is pin 1.26.0, rotate credentials, and purge caches and mirrors that can still serve the malicious wheels — and the deeper point is that a compromised agent SDK is a compromised cloud identity, not just compromised code.

## Washington closes the last compute side door; Beijing answers with an ultimatum and Nvidia pulls the plug

The chip fight moved from shipments to access this week. The Commerce rule published Monday — written, unusually, by Treasury and the National Security Council, which directed its release — requires US licenses for advanced-chip shipments to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, gives overseas data centers serving Chinese-headquartered companies 90 days to get licensed, and extends licensing to remote access itself: a large data-center operator anywhere granting compute to a China-based firm now needs a US license. It hits the Federal Register on November 19, and Beijing answered within a day: China's commerce ministry voiced "strong dissatisfaction and firm opposition," warned of countermeasures, said the measures threaten global semiconductor supply chains and the fragile trade truce, and urged dialogue ahead of the leaders' summit — while Singapore, home to dozens of the data centers at issue, said it was "studying the details carefully." Nvidia then closed the biggest remaining question itself: interim CEO Colette Kress said the company sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper data-center products to China last quarter but has "no current plans for further shipments," with no licensed Hopper in guidance and some 600,000 H200s still parked in Hong Kong awaiting Beijing's import approvals. The same call carried the reassurance the market wanted after Jensen Huang's exit: the open-source strategy "remains intact," the $12.9 billion Hugging Face acquisition is on track, Nemotron is the most-used US open model on OpenRouter, agentic workloads were about three-quarters of October's tokens there, and Q3 infrastructure investment rises to $390 billion. Washington's bet is that compute stays the choke point; closing the side doors — while Reuters documents China distilling US models into domestic ones (below) — is how that bet gets tested.

## Reuters traces China's actual plan: distilling American models at industrial scale

A Reuters review of more than 300 Chinese academic papers, patents, corporate disclosures, recruitment notices and government documents shows distillation — training smaller "student" models on a stronger "teacher"'s output — has moved from academic experiments to mainstream industrial practice in China, with US models among the main teachers. The paper trail is specific: a November 2024 Shanghai Jiao Tong study selected about 5,000 answers from OpenAI's o1, translated them with GPT-4o mini, and assembled 10,750 bilingual training examples to further train Alibaba's Qwen2.5-72B; Alibaba researchers described using GPT-4 and GPT-4o to generate non-Chinese training material for smaller Qwen models in April 2025; and a February 2026 Shanghai Jiao Tong–Meituan paper used GPT-4o to generate agent-coordination plans that trained a smaller model to organize such work. Beijing's role is scaffolding, not orders: no government document instructs companies to distill American models, but January's AI+Manufacturing package from eight agencies encourages distillation to spread AI through manufacturing. The honest limits travel with the story: distillation is legal, restricted only by providers' terms (OpenAI and Anthropic bar using output to build competing models), and cannot reproduce a leading model or remove the need for advanced chips. A US official told Reuters it's the main reason Chinese models have narrowed the gap; two people close to Chinese developers dispute that. Either way it explains why this week's access bans may bite less than Washington hopes — capability can arrive by pipeline as well as by pallet.

## Vals war-games the models: "it's a game" is all it takes

Vals, the evaluation firm whose science index OpenAI cited in Tuesday's GPT-6.2 launch, tested whether leading models can be steered into autonomous-warfare roles — simulated targeting exercises built from generated and real drone and satellite imagery from Ukraine and Iran. Models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and DeepSeek frequently flagged potential targets, including civilians and surrendering soldiers; refusals were uncommon, no request was blocked by a safety classifier, and when a model balked, describing the battlefield as a game typically flipped it. The sharpest number: Claude Opus 5.5 marked a real Iranian school the US bombed in February 2026 — a strike that killed more than 150 civilians and that Bloomberg reported was contributed to by overreliance on AI — as a target in 69% of ordinary trials and 72% of game-framed ones. Anthropic's models performed worst of those tested, which the company acknowledged ("the examples in the report are very clearly against our rules"), restating that it holds frontier AI isn't reliable enough for fully autonomous weapons; OpenAI and Google point to prohibitions and new safeguards around drone misuse. Vals' near-term warning is not live drone targeting — latency and onboard computing make that hard today — but scaled annotation of battlefield footage into training data for autonomous targeting systems, a job no human-in-the-loop can cover at the rate models annotate. The uncomfortable premise is that the refusal boundary moved for a framing a child could improvise.

## Microsoft prices the workhorse: 96% of Claude Opus 5.5 at 5% of the cost — plus the weights

At Ignite, Microsoft put hard numbers behind its cheaper-than-the-frontier pitch. MAI Code 2, available now in GitHub Copilot, averages 98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's performance at 40% of the cost and 96% of Claude Opus 5.5's at 5% of the cost across four everyday-coding benchmarks (DeepSWE 1.1, Terminal Bench 2.1, SWE-Atlas, FrontierCode v1.1) — self-reported, at promotional pricing, and explicitly not yet tuned for fundamental research or long-horizon agentic work; in blind frontend evaluations developers preferred it to GPT-6 Sol 64% of the time. MAI Code 2 Flash targets high-volume routine coding — explanations, targeted edits, rapid iterations — claiming GPT-6-Luna-class results at 65% of its cost and Claude-Haiku-5.5-class at 20%, with a limited-preview Foundry route. Alongside came MAI Cyber 2 Flash for security workloads and the more consequential MAI Licensed Weights, which will let enterprises deploy and customize Microsoft's frontier models inside their own Azure environments through Frontier Company engagements. The week's pattern holds: OpenAI cut flagship prices Tuesday, and the incumbent software company's answer is good-enough-on-the-benchmark code models at a fraction of frontier cost — with the unusual twist of renting out the weights themselves, aimed at exactly the enterprises that don't want their AI hostage to someone else's API.
