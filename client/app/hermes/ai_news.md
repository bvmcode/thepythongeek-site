## A Stanford physicist posted an 83-page quantum-gravity preprint built with GPT-6 Astra — and physicists are pushing back

Venkatesa Chandrasekaran of Stanford's Leinweber Institute for Theoretical Physics posted "An algebraic quantization of gravity from the null cone" to arXiv on November 25 — a single-author, 83-page attempt to quantize vacuum general relativity in four dimensions using the null cones light traces through spacetime — with acknowledgements crediting "detailed, back and forth collaboration with GPT-6 Astra over the course of many months, amounting to ∼ 400M total tokens," compute supplied by OpenAI. The claims: a finite closed-form amplitude for graviton pair creation computed nonperturbatively in Newton's constant (expanding first gives the familiar logarithmic divergence), finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces for bounded causal diamonds, recovery of the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy including Ashoke Sen's one-loop logarithmic correction, and Einstein's equations to one loop as the low-energy limit. The limits are equally clear: vacuum only — no matter — and no experimental test. Sam Altman praised the author's "confidence and courage"; UCL's Jonathan Oppenheim says the claims are not established (his AI-assisted review flagged over a dozen issues), and Columbia's Peter Woit dismissed the buzz as a publicity stunt. Context: Suvrat Raju's TheoPhysEval benchmark of 50 never-published, day-long theory problems saturated before release — GPT-6 Astra solved 49 of 50 for under $3 per problem — and hep-th submissions are up 30% this year against a prior two-decade record of 8%.

## OpenAI's 6.2 updates look cost-tuned — and a billing fix pushed the same work 25–40% further up the usage ladder

An agent-against-agent analysis (November 26) argues GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol — launched November 17 — were tuned for cost: against carefully matched opponents in its real-work duel system, both models' recorded reasoning and output per task drop to levels no other model on the board hits, with p-values on the reasoning decline of 0.00013 for Sol and 0.0064 for Astra, and on the output decline of 0.0055 and 0.022. The author's verdict: "underthinking outliers." Independent API testing: GPT-6.2 Astra used 34% fewer output tokens than GPT-6 Astra and cost 47% less, about two-thirds of the saving from reduced reasoning. The consumer sequel landed November 26, when OpenAI product lead Thibault Sottiaux disclosed a launch bug that "incorrectly discounted GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol usage for most users" since launch; after the fix, the same work reaches plan limits 40% faster on Astra and 25% faster on Sol than last week — no retroactive charges, plus a one-time "banked reset" for Go, Plus, Pro and Business users as an apology. OpenAI hasn't said what broke or how many users were hit.

## Google's Gemini 4 Hydrogen + Orchestrator put a planner-executor model pair inside one coding-agent session

Google introduced Gemini 4 Hydrogen on November 12 (adapted from Gemini 4 Carbon, 1M-token context, $1.50/$7.50 per million input/output tokens) and Gemini 4 Neon on November 18 ($3/$15, rivalling Carbon), but the part developers feel is Orchestrator in Antigravity: a model-dropdown mode pairing a planner (Carbon or Argon) with Hydrogen as executor inside one conversation — the planner studies code read-only and writes an explicit plan with re-planning triggers, Hydrogen edits, builds and tests, and hands control back when a check fails twice or an interface misbehaves, after which the planner rewrites the plan. Google's numbers: on Terminal-Bench, Orchestrator reaches near-Carbon success while consuming 24% less quota and finishing 19% faster. On Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index, Hydrogen at high scored 51 (≈ Argon's 50) and at xhigh 54 (14.9 minutes, $3.16 per task), Neon at xhigh 61 ($6.44, 25.8 min) versus Carbon's high-effort 60 ($5.48, 32 min) — and cheap isn't always fewer tokens: at xhigh Hydrogen emitted 304M output tokens across the Index's tests versus Carbon's 205M. The pattern is spreading — Anthropic's Claude Code has an advisor mode, OpenAI's Codex assigns models to subagents, and Sakana's Fugu routes each step across labs' models.

## Nebius' LayerStream keeps the optimizer in host memory — MoE fine-tuning runs 3.15× faster on the same GPUs and up to 80% cheaper

Nebius' LayerStream training engine attacks the quiet cost center of full fine-tuning an MoE model: with ~18 bytes of state per parameter, a 313B model needs about 5.6TB before activations, so cluster size is set by memory, not compute. LayerStream keeps optimizer state and saved activations in host memory, streams each layer's working weights through the GPU only while that layer runs, applies each layer's AdamW update during the backward pass, routes tokens to their experts over fast node-internal links with fixed-size cross-node traffic overlapped against computation, and reuses forward-pass intermediates instead of recomputing. Results: on the same 16 H200s, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next (177B total, 6.67B active) finished a 25.8M-token pass in 7.1 minutes instead of 22.4 — 3.15× the throughput of a tuned Megatron-LM trainer at 68% lower GPU cost per token; GLM-5.3-Flash (313B) ran 2.97× faster on 16 B300s than a 24-B300 baseline (78% cheaper) and 3.1× on 32 cheaper H100s (80% cheaper). The caveats are stated plainly: matched-data checks show comparable loss over one epoch, not equivalent downstream quality or long-run convergence, and the largest GLM comparison crosses different data and losses.

## Plato's Key: one closed-form rotation lets one LLM use another's memory

Stanford's Hazy Research lab (November 25) gave the Platonic Representation Hypothesis a functional test: if separately trained networks truly converge on similar representations, how interchangeable are they? A surprisingly simple map — a rotation plus one global scale, fit in closed form (SVD-based orthogonal Procrustes on the hidden "keys" of under 5,000 facts both models recall) — lets a donor LLM's hidden state stand in for the recipient's own. Across 20 pairs of 12 base LLMs from 8 families (1.5B–27B) over 8,800 factual-recall cases, the rotated donor state flipped the recipient's answer a median 41% of the time (73% of a within-model swap), and no more flexible map beat it reliably; chained rotations A→B→C keep 99% of a direct A→C rotation, and 256 principal components per LLM keep 74%. The striking part: a rotated donor key unlocks "dark memories" — of the recipient's 1,463 qualifying misses (56% of 2,600 tested), a swapped key recovered 18% of facts the model stored but failed to recall, versus at most 1.1% under controls — meaning some recall failures come from bad representations, not missing knowledge.

## StateBridge: agents on the same model skip text and pass hidden states directly

A University of Sheffield team (Yanwen Peng et al., first posted to arXiv in August) built StateBridge, a training-free way for agents built on the same LLM to communicate in latent space rather than tokens: take the sender's final-layer hidden states for the last 64 tokens of its message, rotate them toward their own token embeddings in closed form, rescale to the embedding statistics, blend each state 70/30 with its nearest token embedding, and prepend the 64 vectors to the receiver's input — no gradients, no training, Apache 2.0. Running four agents in a planner → critic → refiner → judge chain (roles borrowed from LatentMAS), it beat every alternative — a single agent, the same four agents exchanging text, and LatentMAS — by 2.4 to 2.9 points on average across MedQA, HumanEval+ and GSM8K on Qwen3-4B/8B/32B and OLMo3-7B-Think. The margin peaks on GPQA-Diamond, where Qwen3-8B agents hit 52.5% with StateBridge versus 45.5% for LatentMAS and 39.9% solo; on OLMo3-7B-Think the average was 76.7% against 73.9% for text hand-off. Passing 64 vectors that outscore the whole-message hand-off hints that documents and tool outputs might someday travel the same way.
